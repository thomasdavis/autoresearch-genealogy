# Wagadagam Clan

The Wagadagam (also written Wagedogam or Wagedoegam) is a clan and village territory on **Mabuiag Island** in the western Torres Strait. The Wagadagam people are part of the broader Gumuligal moiety of Mabuiag and their primary totem is the **crocodile (Koedal)**, with the **northwest wind** as their directional association.

Royston Sagigi-Baira, winner of Australian Idol 2023, identifies as a Wagadagam (Torres Strait Islander) and Thanakwith (Aboriginal) man.

## Location: Mabuiag Island

Mabuiag Island lies in the Bellevue Islands group, approximately 100 kilometres north of Thursday Island in the western Torres Strait. The island measures 6.3 square kilometres and has been continuously inhabited for at least **7,300 years**, based on archaeological excavations.

The past 1,000 years saw expanded settlement patterns on Mabuiag. During the past 400 to 500 years, large, highly structured mounds of dugong bone, as well as shell and stone arrangements, provide evidence for emerging totemic divisions. This archaeological record demonstrates that the totem-based clan system, including the Wagadagam, developed and formalised over several centuries.

Evidence of pottery, typically associated with Melanesian peoples, appears at two sites on Mabuiag: Mui (east coast) and Mask Cave (adjacent Pulu islet), dating to approximately 2,000 years ago.

## Moiety System

Mabuiag's social structure is organised into two **moieties** (complementary halves):

| Moiety | Clan Territory | Location on Island | Key Totem |
|--------|---------------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Gumuligal** | Wagadagam | Northwest side | Crocodile (Koedal) |
| **Mabuygilgal** | Paipaidagam | Southeast side | (various) |

This dual organisation reflects a spatial division of the island into complementary clan territories, each with distinct totemic affiliations, ceremonial roles, and resource management responsibilities.

## The Crocodile Totem (Koedal)

The crocodile (Koedal) is the primary totem of the Wagadagam people. In Torres Strait Islander culture, totems are far more than symbolic emblems. They define:

- **Kinship relationships**: Who you can marry, who you are obligated to support
- **Connection to land and sea**: Specific territories, reefs, and marine areas associated with the clan
- **Ceremonial roles**: Responsibilities in ritual and community governance
- **Resource management**: Obligations regarding the totem species and its habitat

The crocodile is a major totem in the **Muluilgal Nation** (the collective identity of the Western Island Groups of Torres Strait). The saltwater crocodile is a powerful presence in Torres Strait waters and has deep spiritual significance across the western islands.

Artist Alick Tipoti of Badu Island, whose totems include the crocodile, has produced significant artworks depicting Koedal. At Badu Art Centre, crocodile imagery features prominently in linocut prints and other artistic works that preserve and transmit totemic knowledge.

## Wind and Seasonal Association

Each Torres Strait clan is associated with specific winds, connecting people to the seasonal rhythms of the strait. The Wagadagam's association with the northwest wind places them within the seasonal cycle that governs fishing, gardening, and ceremonial activities in the western Torres Strait.

The wind associations form a complete "wind circle" when all clans are considered together, mapping the social structure onto the natural environment. This system is recorded in detail for the Meriam clans of the eastern islands and operates analogously in the western islands.

## Villages of Mabuiag

Historical records identify three ethnographically significant villages on Mabuiag:

1. **Goemu**
2. **Wagadagam** (Wagedoegam)
3. **Dhabangay**

These villages were formed over the last 1,000 years as settlement patterns expanded across the island.

## Historical Context

The Mabuiag people historically sustained themselves through fishing and hunting dugong. They maintained a reputation for hostility toward outsiders until accepting Christianity in the early 1870s, following the broader pattern of the Coming of the Light across Torres Strait.

The traditional language of Mabuiag is **Kalaw Lagaw Ya** (specifically the Mabuyaagi dialect), the same language family spoken on Badu Island. This shared language connects the Wagadagam people to the broader western Torres Strait cultural sphere.

## Connection to the Sagigi-Baira Family

Royston Sagigi-Baira (born 25 December 1998) is a Thanakwith (Aboriginal) and Wagadagam (Torres Strait Islander) man from Mapoon, Queensland. His dual cultural heritage reflects the interconnections between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities in the Cape York region. His father's lineage traces to the Torres Strait Islander community.

The Baira family has deep roots in Badu Island's governance. Jacob Baira represented Badu at the first Inter Islander Councillors Conference in 1937. Tom Jack Ebod Baira (Bahdu) served as a Wakaid Tribal Councillor.

## Mabuiag Today

**Population (2021 census)**: 253 residents, with 94.1% identifying as Indigenous Australians.

Mabuiag is served by:
- Tagai State College, Mabuiag Island campus (Early Childhood through Year 6)
- Mabuiag Island Airport (450-metre runway, reportedly the shortest in Australia for commercial services)
- Ngalpun Ngulaygaw Lag Resource Centre (Indigenous Knowledge Centre with public library)
- Magistrates' Court (opened April 2010, operating on the Torres Strait Court Circuit)
