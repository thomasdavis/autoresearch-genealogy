# Torres Strait Light Infantry Battalion

The **Torres Strait Light Infantry Battalion** was an infantry battalion of the Australian Army during the Second World War. It was the **only Indigenous Australian battalion ever formed** by the Australian Army, with almost all of its enlisted men being Torres Strait Islanders. The battalion's formation, service, and the discrimination its members faced represent a significant chapter in both Torres Strait Islander and Australian military history.

Elia Doolah served as a Private in the Torres Strait Light Infantry Battalion.

## Formation

The unit was initially formed as an **independent infantry company** of just over 100 men in **May 1941**, after the Australian Government became concerned about the possibility of conflict in the Pacific and sought to free up other units for service elsewhere.

On **1 March 1943**, the unit was officially expanded to a full battalion under the command of **Major John Uther (Jock) Swain**, with an authorised strength of 17 officers and 470 men.

## Recruitment

The response to recruitment was extraordinary:

- **830 Torres Strait Islander men** joined the ranks throughout the course of the war
- An additional **40 Torres Strait Malays and Aboriginal people** were also recruited
- The total number who enlisted was equal to approximately **one-fifth of the entire population** of the Torres Strait at the time
- This represented almost every eligible man in the region

The scale of enlistment demonstrated the Torres Strait Islander community's commitment to defending their homeland, despite the discriminatory conditions under which they served.

## Organisation

The battalion was organised into:

- **Four rifle companies**: Designated A, B, C, and D
- **Pioneer company**: 160 men, formed May 1943
- **Transportation company**: Established mid-1944

Companies were spread across multiple locations in the Torres Strait.

## Commanding Officers

| Period | Commander |
|--------|----------|
| 1942-1944 | Major John Uther (Jock) Swain |
| 1944-1946 | Major Charles Frederick Mayne Godtschalk |

Due to the racial policies in place at the time, **all officers and senior non-commissioned officers were white Australians**, while Torres Strait Islanders served as enlisted men and junior NCOs.

## Deployment and Operations

### Garrison Defence

The battalion's primary role was the **garrison defence** of the Torres Strait Islands at the northern tip of Queensland. Companies were stationed across:

- **Horn Island**: A key defensive position (Horn Island was bombed by Japanese aircraft during the war)
- **Goode Island**
- **Thursday Island**: Military headquarters for Torres Strait operations

The battalion formed part of **Torres Strait Force** and served as an integral component of the islands' defences against Japanese air raids.

Badu Island hosted the **341 radar station**, which monitored Japanese aircraft movements, with some battalion members assigned to radar station security.

### Dutch New Guinea Patrol (1943)

In **October to December 1943**, a detachment was deployed to **Dutch New Guinea** (now Papua province, Indonesia) for patrol operations. This was the only deployment outside the Torres Strait.

### Combat Action

On **23 December 1943**, the detachment in Dutch New Guinea had a skirmish with Japanese forces:
- **One killed**
- **Six wounded**

This was the battalion's only significant engagement with enemy forces.

### Consolidation (Mid-1944)

By mid-1944, outposts across the Torres Strait were deemed unnecessary as the threat of Japanese invasion receded. The battalion was consolidated on **Thursday Island**.

## The Pay Dispute and Strike

The pay dispute is one of the most significant events in the battalion's history and a landmark moment in Indigenous Australian activism.

### The Discrimination

Torres Strait Islander soldiers initially received **one-third of the pay** of white soldiers of equal rank. This was consistent with the broader pattern of economic discrimination against Torres Strait Islanders under Queensland's protection legislation.

### The Strike

In **late December 1943**, members of **A, B, and C Companies** conducted a **brief strike action**, refusing to work until pay conditions were improved. This was a remarkably bold action during wartime, when military discipline was strictly enforced.

The strike echoed the 1936 civilian strike by Torres Strait Islander workers, demonstrating a continuing pattern of organised resistance to discriminatory policies.

### Resolution

In **February 1944**, the Army agreed to increase the soldiers' pay to **two-thirds** of that of white soldiers. While an improvement, this still represented significant discrimination.

### Full Back Pay

The Indigenous soldiers finally received **full back pay** for their war service in **1986**, more than four decades after the war ended. This belated recognition acknowledged the injustice of the original pay arrangements.

## Casualties

A total of **36 members** of the battalion died on active service during the war.

## Disbandment

The battalion was **disbanded in 1946** following the end of hostilities. After the war, many veterans returned to their island communities, while others moved to the mainland, contributing to the broader pattern of Torres Strait Islander migration.

## Legacy

### Military Significance

The Torres Strait Light Infantry Battalion holds a unique place in Australian military history as the only all-Indigenous battalion ever formed. The near-universal enlistment of eligible Torres Strait Islander men (one-fifth of the entire population) represents one of the highest per-capita military participation rates of any community in Australian history.

### Civil Rights Significance

The battalion's 1943 pay strike, following the 1936 civilian strike, established a pattern of organised Torres Strait Islander resistance to discriminatory government policies. This activism would continue through the post-war decades and ultimately contribute to the broader Indigenous rights movement.

### Post-War Impact

The experience of military service had profound effects on Torres Strait Islander communities:

- Veterans gained experience of organisation, discipline, and collective action
- The discrimination they faced strengthened determination to fight for equal rights
- Military service gave Islanders a powerful moral claim to full citizenship
- The post-war decline of the pearling industry, combined with military experience, accelerated migration to the mainland

### Commemoration

- **Community marks anniversary**: The Defence community regularly commemorates the battalion's formation
- **Last surviving member**: Mebai Warusam, the last surviving member of the battalion, died in **July 2023**
- The battalion is honoured at the Australian War Memorial and in Torres Strait Islander communities

## Connection to Broader WWII Torres Strait Operations

The Torres Strait was a strategically important area during WWII:

- Japanese aircraft bombed **Horn Island** and surrounding areas
- The strait was a potential invasion route from Japanese-held New Guinea
- Australian and U.S. forces maintained significant garrisons across the island chain
- The **341 radar station** on Badu Island monitored Japanese aircraft movements
- Thursday Island served as the military headquarters for the region
