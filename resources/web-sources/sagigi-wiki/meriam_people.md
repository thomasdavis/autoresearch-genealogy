# Meriam People

The Meriam are an Indigenous Australian group of Torres Strait Islander people who inhabit the outer eastern Torres Strait Islands, including Mer (Murray Island), Ugar (Stephen Island), and Erub (Darnley Island). They are united by a common language (Meriam Mir), strong ties of kinship, and a shared tradition as skilled hunter-fisher-gatherers living in family groups or clans.

The Meriam are of **Melanesian origin**, distinct from the western Torres Strait Islanders who speak the Pama-Nyungan language Kala Lagaw Ya. The Meriam's Papuan linguistic and cultural connections link them to the peoples of southern New Guinea.

The Doolah family is Meriam, specifically of the **Samsep-Meriam** clan.

## Language: Meriam Mir

Meriam Mir is the traditional language of the eastern Torres Strait Islands. It is classified within the **Eastern Trans-Fly language family**, making it the only Papuan language spoken on Australian territory.

Key facts about Meriam Mir:

- **Classification**: Originally placed in the Trans-New Guinea Phylum by Stephen Wurm, though Malcolm Ross concluded in 2005 that the Eastern Trans-Fly languages were not part of Trans-New Guinea
- **Grammar**: Features agglutinative morphology and double-marking alignment, where verbs cross-reference arguments for person, number, and syntactic function
- **Speakers**: 217 recorded in the 2016 Australian census (up from 186 in 2011)
- **Status**: Classified as endangered by UNESCO
- **Current use**: On Mer Island, 23.6% of residents spoke Meriam Mir in 2016, while 52.6% spoke Yumplatok (Torres Strait Creole)

The language shares cognates with Trans-Fly languages in personal pronouns and verbal/nominal morphology, reflecting the Meriam people's Papuan origins.

## The Eight Tribes of Mer

Mer Island is home to eight tribes (clans), each with defined territorial boundaries extending from the land out to the edge of the home reef. Until the 1890s, the eight clans lived in clusters of villages within these clan territories, spread around the islands of Mer, Dauar, and Waier.

Within each clan, "nameholders" maintain ownership of particular plots of land, reefs, lagoons, and stone fish-traps, responsible for them on behalf of their family.

### Clan Directory

| Clan | Season/Wind | Totems |
|------|-------------|--------|
| **Mei-Zagareb** | Northeast season and wind | Seagull, Torres Strait pigeon |
| **Meauram** | Northeast season, north wind | Turtle |
| **Komet** | Northwest season and wind | Sardine, mangrove |
| **Peibre** | Northwest season, west northwest wind | Manta ray |
| **Giar Dauareb** | West southwest wind | Frigate bird, mackerel |
| **Teg Dauareb** | South southwest wind | Mackerel, turtle |
| **Teg Magaram** | Southeast season, south wind | Whale, snake |
| **Geauram** | Southeast season, south wind | Whale, snake, dove |
| **Samsep/Meriam** | Southeast season, east southeast wind | Tiger shark, Torres Strait pigeon, driftwood |
| **Piadram** | (sub-clan of Samsep) | Tiger shark, Torres Strait pigeon, driftwood, whale |

All eight tribes share the **shark** as a collective protective totem.

A key cultural division exists between the eastern and western clans: the two easterly Samsep groups are "garden people," while the western territorial groups are "fish groupings." In the Malo ceremonies, the garden groupings are the dancers, while the fish groupings are the song men.

### The Samsep-Meriam Clan

The Samsep-Meriam clan (also written Meriam-samsep) occupies the southeastern portion of Mer. Their totems include the tiger shark, Torres Strait pigeon, and driftwood. The sub-clan Piadram shares these totems and additionally claims the whale.

The Samsep are classified as "garden people" within the Meriam cultural framework, indicating their primary economic orientation toward agriculture rather than fishing. In ceremonial contexts, particularly the Malo-Bomai rituals, the Samsep serve as dancers.

The Doolah family belongs to the Samsep-Meriam clan.

## Traditional Culture

### The Law of Malo

Before Christianity, the Meriam followed **Malo**, a system of law and ceremony with principles and structure similar to Christianity. The story of Malo tells of one of four brothers who sailed from New Guinea. A strong wind separated them, and Malo sailed alone until a big wave sank his canoe at Mer. After arriving, Malo gave the Meriam laws and ceremonies to govern their lives.

The Malo-Bomai religious and ceremonial system governed social behaviour, land use, and inter-clan relations. Its structural similarity to Christianity facilitated the Meriam people's adoption of the new religion after 1871.

### Agriculture and Sea Rights

Gardening takes priority in Meriam subsistence, with fathers transmitting gardening techniques to sons across generations. Each Meriam family also holds sea rights and maintains:

- Stone fish-weirs on the reefs
- Crayfish holes
- Specific reef and lagoon territories

This dual land-sea economy reflects the Meriam's adaptation to their volcanic island environment.

### Death Rites and Maier

Maier (bright meteors) play central roles in Meriam death rituals. They inform communities about deaths and enable the spirits of the dead to communicate with the living.

### Creation Stories

Key Meriam creation narratives include:

- **Nageg and Geigi**: A mother and son who transform into triggerfish and trevally, explaining the spiritual importance of sea country
- **Gelam**: A boy whose journey created the three islands of Mer, Waier, and Dauar

## The Coming of the Light

On 1 July 1871, representatives of the London Missionary Society arrived at Erub (Darnley Island) aboard the schooner Surprise, accompanied by eight Lifu (Loyalty Islander) evangelists and their wives. The Reverends Samuel Macfarlane and Archibald Wright Murray presented the Bible to Dabad, a clan elder, who accepted the gift, interpreted as the "Light."

Christianity spread rapidly through the eastern islands. The Meriam accepted it relatively easily due to the structural similarities between Malo and Christian teachings. The Coming of the Light is celebrated annually on 1 July across all Torres Strait communities, representing a coming together of clans and cultures.

Today, Anglicanism (45.7% on Mer) and Pentecostalism (10%) are the predominant denominations. Indigenous spirituality and Christian spirituality coexist among the Meriam.

## The Mabo Case

The Mabo case (Mabo v Queensland No 2) is the most significant legal event in Meriam history and one of the most important in Australian law.

### Timeline

- **20 May 1982**: Eddie Koiki Mabo, Sam Passi, David Passi, Celuia Mapo Salee, and James Rice lodged a case with the High Court of Australia for legal ownership of Mer
- **1982-1992**: Over 10 years, 33 Meriam people generated 4,000 pages of transcripts of evidence, proving that the eight clans had occupied clearly defined territories for hundreds of years and demonstrating the continuity of Meriam custom
- **3 June 1992**: The High Court ruled 6-1 in favour of the Meriam, recognising their traditional ownership of the lands of Mer

### Significance

The decision:

- Rejected the doctrine of **terra nullius** (the legal fiction that Australia was unowned at the time of British colonisation)
- Recognised that native title existed by virtue of traditional customs and laws and had not been wholly lost upon colonisation
- Established that rights arising under native title were recognised within Australia's common law
- Led directly to the **Native Title Act 1993**, providing the framework for all Indigenous Australians to make native title claims
- By 2022, over 500 successful native title claims had been made, with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander rights recognised across 40% of the Australian land mass

### The Plaintiffs

1. **Eddie Koiki Mabo** (lead plaintiff, died 21 January 1992, five months before the decision)
2. **Reverend David Passi**
3. **Sam Passi**
4. **James Rice**
5. **Celuia Mapo Salee**

## Historical Events

### European Contact

- **1791**: Captain Edwards of HMS Pandora named the island "Murray Island"
- **1802**: Matthew Flinders mapped the area, describing residents as "friendly and eager for trade"

### Colonial Economy

From the 1860s onward, beche-de-mer and pearling industries transformed the eastern islands. Douglas Pitt established operations around 1871 but was evicted in 1882 for unlawful occupation. The Papuan Industrial School, established by Reverend Samuel MacFarlane around 1879, taught both religious instruction and trades like boat building.

### Government Control

By 1912, 1,200 acres of Mer were gazetted as an Aboriginal reserve. The Queensland Government increasingly controlled Islander labour and movement. In the 1936 strike, Mer's Chief Councillor Marou Mimi led resistance against government interference, with approximately 70% of the Torres Strait Islander workforce participating.

### Scientific Documentation

In 1898, Alfred Court Haddon led the Cambridge University expedition, spending seven months documenting Torres Strait Islander culture. The expedition produced the first sound recordings and films of Torres Strait Islander people.

### World War II

Meriam men served in the Torres Strait Light Infantry Battalion, receiving one-third pay of white servicemen. Following a 1943 strike, pay was raised to two-thirds; full back pay was offered in the 1980s.

### Land Rights

Mer initially declined a Deed of Grant in Trust from the Queensland Government, asserting that the land was not the government's to give. Mer finally accepted a deed in December 2012 for the islands of Mer, Waier, and Dauar.

## Notable Meriam People

- **Eddie Koiki Mabo** (1936-1992): Lead plaintiff in the Mabo case
- **Reverend David Passi**: Mabo case plaintiff
- **George Mye**: Prominent Indigenous rights advocate from Erub (1960s-1990s)
- **Carlemo Wacando**: Challenged terra nullius doctrine
- **Dr Vanessa Lee**: Senior lecturer in behavioural and social sciences, University of Sydney
- **Thelma Weston**: Named Female Elder of the Year for nursing/health work
- **Wasie Tardent and Clinton Naina**: Contemporary artists narrating storytelling traditions
