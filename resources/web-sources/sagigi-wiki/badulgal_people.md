# Badulgal People

The Badulgal are the Torres Strait Islander people of Badu Island (also known as Mulgrave Island), located in the central-western Torres Strait, approximately 60 kilometres north of Thursday Island, Queensland, Australia. The Badulgal are of Melanesian descent and are the traditional owners of Badu and its surrounding waters. Together with the neighbouring Mualgal people, they are collectively referred to as the **Mura Badulgal**, a term that acknowledges all clans inclusively.

The Sagigi family identifies as Badulgal.

## Language

The Badulgal speak **Kala Lagaw Ya** (also written Kalaw Lagaw Ya), the traditional language of the western and central Torres Strait Islands. Kala Lagaw Ya belongs to the Pama-Nyungan language family, making it linguistically related to mainland Australian Aboriginal languages rather than the Papuan languages spoken in the eastern Torres Strait.

Kala Lagaw Ya is a mixed language with a Pama-Nyungan grammatical base overlaid with substantial Papuan (Trans-Fly) and Austronesian vocabulary. Estimates suggest up to 80% of its vocabulary is non-Australian in origin. Before colonisation in the 1870s and 1880s, Kala Lagaw Ya served as the major lingua franca of the entire Torres Strait cultural area, extending from northern Cape York to the coast of Papua New Guinea's Western Province.

The language has four dialects, determined by geography and shaped over time by trade, inter-marriage, and kinship ties: Kalau Lagau Ya, Kalau Kawau Ya, Kulkalgau Ya, and Kaiwaligau Ya. These dialects are mutually intelligible. Today approximately 4,000 people speak the language, though fluent speakers of the older generation are diminishing. Artists like Alick Tipoti, himself a Badulgal man from Badu, are among the last generation to speak Kala Lagaw Ya fluently and are actively involved in language preservation programs.

The language features a split-ergativity grammatical system and distinguishes between inclusive and exclusive first-person pronouns (including dual forms), a feature consistent with its Australian linguistic heritage.

## Totem and Clan System

Torres Strait Islander society is organised around totemic clans (augud). Totems form the foundation of cultural identity and can be living creatures, rocks, winds, or stars. A person's totem determines kinship relationships, obligations, and connections to specific areas of land and sea.

The Badulgal are part of the broader Muluilgal Nation (Western Island Groups). Key totems in the western Torres Strait include the crocodile (Koedal), dugong, turtle, and shark. The crocodile totem (Koedal) is particularly significant in the western islands, belonging to the Wagadagam clan associated with Mabuiag Island and the broader western island cultural sphere.

Each clan is also associated with specific winds and directional orientations, connecting people to the seasonal rhythms of the Torres Strait environment.

## Traditional Life and Governance

Before European contact, the Badulgal maintained village-based communities focused on:

- **Hunting and fishing**: The surrounding waters, with their complex tides reaching up to 7 knots, provided rich marine resources
- **Farming**: Garden cultivation was a core subsistence activity
- **Canoe building**: Essential for inter-island travel and trade
- **Inter-island trade**: Regular exchange networks connected Badu with neighbouring islands
- **Warfare**: The Badulgal had a reputation as fierce warriors. Headhunting was a widespread practice throughout the Torres Strait until the 1870s. The ritual involved a cane hoop and a special bamboo knife (upi) for severing the head, which was then boiled and dressed with beeswax noses and eyes fashioned from nautilus nacre.

Governance was clan-based, with custodianship of land and sea shared among clan groups. The Mura Badulgal system of collective custodianship reflects deep kinship networks built around maritime adaptation.

## European Contact

- **1605**: Willem Janszoon sailed near Badu in the Duyfken en route to the East Indies, describing the region as "a waste land populated by cruel savages."
- **1606**: Portuguese navigator Luis Vaz de Torres navigated through the strait that now bears his name.
- **1792**: Captain William Bligh mapped Torres Strait during his second breadfruit voyage, naming Badu "Mulgrave Island" after warning shots were fired at approaching canoes.
- **June 1846**: Badu islanders killed three Europeans from the Thomas Lord, a vessel anchored while searching for trepang (sea cucumbers).

## Christianisation and the Coming of the Light

The London Missionary Society's arrival at Erub (Darnley Island) on 1 July 1871, known as "the Coming of the Light," marked the beginning of Christianity's spread through the Torres Strait. After earlier failed attempts in the 1870s, Badu residents welcomed missionaries in 1884. The adoption of Christianity effectively ended warfare and headhunting practices.

Torres Strait Islanders celebrate the Coming of the Light annually on 1 July. The event is understood not merely as a religious conversion but as a coming together of clans and cultures and a strengthening of family and community. Islanders maintained their traditional customs and cultural identity (Ailan Kastom / Aislan Kustom) alongside Christian practice.

## Pearl Shell Industry

The pearl shell and beche-de-mer (sea cucumber) industries transformed Badu's economy from the 1870s onward:

- **1870s**: Pearlers established bases on Badu Island
- **Early 1880s**: Islanders became dependent on pearling wages
- **Late 1890s**: Reverend Walker established Papuan Industries Limited (PIL), promoting cooperative pearl lugger ownership among Islanders
- **1904**: PIL's main headquarters opened at Badu, making it the administrative centre of the cooperative enterprise
- **1906**: Islanders purchased their first company boat
- **1930**: Queensland Government took over PIL, renaming it the Aboriginal Industries Board
- **Peak (late 1950s)**: The Badu fleet comprised 13 boats employing a workforce of 200. The Nona family, led by Tanu Nona, controlled eight government vessels with diving gear and accounted for over 50% of the government fleet's catch.
- **Post-1950s decline**: The development of plastics undercut demand for pearl shell buttons, devastating the island economy and prompting migration to the mainland

## Government Control and the 1936 Strike

After the death of John Douglas in 1904, the Queensland Government increasingly restricted Islander movement, labour, and access to savings under the Aboriginal Protection and Restriction of the Sale of Opium Act 1897. In November 1912, 22,000 acres at Badu were gazetted as an Aboriginal reserve. The Torres Strait Islanders Act 1939 formally recognised Torres Strait Islanders as a separate people from Aboriginal Australians.

In 1936, approximately 70% of the Torres Strait Islander workforce struck for nine months, the first organised challenge to government authority. Strikers demanded:

- Wage recognition
- Trade autonomy
- Removal of curfew
- Elimination of inter-island travel permits
- Crew recruitment rights

Approximately 30 men were imprisoned on Badu for non-compliance. The strike succeeded in removing the unpopular Protector J.D. McLean, who was replaced by Cornelius O'Leary. O'Leary established regular consultations with elected Island Council representatives.

Between 1939 and 1950, seventeen documented removals occurred from Badu to mainland reserves.

## World War II

Torres Strait Islander men formed the Torres Strait Light Infantry Battalion, the only Indigenous Australian battalion ever raised. Badu hosted the 341 radar station, which monitored Japanese aircraft movements. Islander soldiers received only one-third of white servicemen's pay. On 31 December 1943, they struck for equal compensation; pay was eventually increased to two-thirds. Full back pay was not offered until the 1980s.

## Native Title and Land Rights

The Badulgal people's struggle for land recognition dates back to 1939. On 1 February 2014, the Queensland Government handed over freehold title to 9,836 hectares (24,310 acres) of land to the Badulgal traditional owners, ending decades of campaigning. The Mura Badulgal (Torres Strait Islanders) Corporation now administers land on behalf of the Badulgal people as a registered native title body corporate.

## Art and Culture

Badu Island has gained international recognition for its art, particularly linocut printmaking. The Badhulgaw Kuthinaw Mudh Art Centre displays and sells local artwork featuring bold imagery drawn from marine life, ancestral stories, winds, and constellations. The art expresses Aislan Kustom, the spirit of Badu.

Notable Badu artists include:

- **Alick Tipoti** (born 1975, traditional name Zugub): Internationally recognised artist, linguist, and activist. His totems are File-ray and Crocodile; his winds are South Easterly and North Easterly. He works across painting, installations, printmaking, sculpture, and mask-making, focused on preserving Badulgal culture and Kala Lagaw Ya language.
- **Laurie Nona**: Another internationally recognised artist from Badu.

## Notable Badulgal People

- **Tanu Nona** (c.1902-1980): Born on Saibai Island, became Badu's dominant pearler and politician. Elected to the Badu council in 1929, he directed construction of the island's church (completed January 1936) and dominated local government until his retirement in 1976. Appointed O.B.E. in 1972.
- **Jacob Baira**: Represented Badu at the first Inter Islander Councillors Conference in 1937.
- **David Fifita, Dane Gagai, Brenko Lee, Edrick Lee**: Rugby league footballers of Badulgal heritage.
- **Tallisha Harden, Treymain Spry**: Additional sporting figures from Badu.

## Community Today

**Population (2021 census)**: 704 people, with 86.9% identifying as Indigenous Australians.

**Wakaid** is the only town, located on the southeast coast. The settlement also includes Dogai, on the southeastern coast facing Moa Island.

Key facilities include:
- St Mark's Church (constructed 1933-1935, accommodates approximately 700 people)
- Badu Island campus of Tagai State College (originally opened 29 January 1905)
- Badu Island Indigenous Knowledge Centre
- Health centre with permanent doctor
- Two grocery stores, post office, Centrelink agency
- Regional council office
- Football field and motel
- Live seafood export businesses
- 500-metre airstrip

## Torres Strait Treaty

Papua New Guinea's independence in 1975 led to the Torres Strait Treaty (signed December 1978, operational February 1985). The treaty established boundaries and special provisions for a Protected Zone, allowing free movement for traditional fishing, trading, and family gatherings without passports.
