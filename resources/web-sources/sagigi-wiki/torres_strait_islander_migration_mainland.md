# Torres Strait Islander Migration to Mainland Australia

The migration of Torres Strait Islander peoples from their island homelands to mainland Australia is one of the most significant demographic shifts in Indigenous Australian history. Beginning in the early 20th century and accelerating dramatically in the 1960s and 1970s, this movement brought thousands of Islanders to cities including Cairns, Townsville, and Brisbane, as well as to remote mining regions in Western Australia. Today, significantly more Torres Strait Islanders live on the mainland than in the Torres Strait itself.

Families like the Doolahs were part of this migration wave, relocating from the Torres Strait to mainland Queensland communities.

## Scale of the Migration

The demographic transformation has been dramatic:

- Approximately **6,000** Torres Strait Islanders currently live in the Torres Strait region
- Approximately **37,000** live outside the region, primarily in coastal towns of north Queensland
- The largest mainland concentrations are in **Townsville** and **Cairns**
- By the mid-1970s, **half of the entire Islander population** had left Torres Strait
- Today, many more Torres Strait Islanders live on the mainland than on the islands

## Push Factors

### Collapse of the Pearl Shell Industry

The single most important economic push factor was the collapse of the pearl shell industry:

- In the **1950s**, the development of plastic buttons destroyed the market for pearl shell
- The Torres Strait had supplied over 50% of the world's pearl shell at the industry's peak in the 1890s
- Pearl diving had been the primary cash economy for Islanders since the 1870s
- By the late 1950s, the industry was in terminal decline
- The Badu fleet, which at its peak comprised 13 boats employing 200 workers, lost its economic base

### WWII Disruption

World War II had already disrupted the pearl industry:
- Luggers were requisitioned by the Australian Navy
- The Torres Strait became a military zone
- Normal economic activity was suspended
- After the war, the Aboriginal Industries Board headquarters relocated from Badu to Thursday Island

### Government Control

The restrictive governance of the Queensland Protection Board pushed some Islanders to seek independence on the mainland:
- Movement between islands required government permits
- Wages were controlled and savings held in government trust accounts
- Employment options were limited to government-approved activities
- On the mainland, these restrictions did not apply (or were less enforceable)

### Limited Services

The islands offered limited:
- Secondary and tertiary education (students had to travel to Thursday Island or the mainland)
- Healthcare beyond basic services
- Employment diversity
- Housing and infrastructure

## Pull Factors

### Employment Opportunities

The mainland offered diverse employment unavailable in the Torres Strait:

#### Railway Construction ("Railway Time")

The most iconic migration pathway was through railway construction:

- In the **1960s**, young Torres Strait Islander men moved to the mainland to work on railway construction, particularly in Western Australia
- This period is commemorated in Islander communities as **"railway time"**
- On **29 January 1965**, approximately 40 Thursday Islanders arrived in Perth to begin work on the standard-gauge railway project
- In Western Australia, Islanders were part of a multicultural workforce comprising Yugoslavs, Greeks, Italians, and Portuguese
- Torres Strait Islanders were considered some of the **best tracklayers in the world**
- In **1968**, a Torres Strait Islander track-laying team set a world record at Mt Newman

#### Advantages of Railway Work

Railway employment offered specific advantages over remaining in the Torres Strait:
- **Union membership**: As railway workers, Islanders were union members with access to fair contracts
- **Equal wages**: Unlike in the Torres Strait, mainland workers received standard award wages
- **Freedom from the Protection Board**: The strict governance of the Protection Board did not extend to mainland employment

#### Other Employment

Islanders also found work in:
- Maritime and port industries
- Construction
- Government services
- The armed forces (building on WWII service experience)
- Various urban industries

### Education

Mainland cities offered access to:
- Secondary schooling
- Technical and further education (TAFE)
- University education
- Vocational training

### Healthcare

Major hospitals and specialist medical services were available in Cairns, Townsville, and Brisbane, far exceeding what island health centres could provide.

## Migration Patterns by Destination

### Cairns

Torres Strait Islander migration to Cairns began before World War II, with the city serving as the nearest major mainland centre to the Torres Strait. Cairns was the natural first stop for Islanders travelling south, as shipping routes connected Thursday Island to Cairns. The Torres Strait Islander community in Cairns grew steadily through the mid-20th century.

### Townsville

Townsville became one of the largest concentrations of Torres Strait Islanders on the mainland. Its position as a major regional city in North Queensland, with a military base, port, and diverse economy, made it attractive to migrating families.

### Brisbane

As the Queensland state capital, Brisbane attracted Islanders seeking:
- Government employment
- Higher education
- Specialised medical care
- Broader economic opportunities

The distance from the Torres Strait (over 2,000 kilometres) made Brisbane migrations more permanent than moves to Cairns or Townsville.

### Western Australia (Pilbara and Railway Towns)

The railway construction boom of the 1960s drew Torres Strait Islander workers to remote Western Australian communities:
- Some workers married local women and settled in the **Pilbara**
- Families built and maintained a sense of "islanderness" in these remote mainland communities
- The Pilbara Torres Strait Islander community became a distinctive diaspora group

## Community Formation on the Mainland

### Maintaining "Islanderness"

Migrating Torres Strait Islanders worked actively to maintain their cultural identity on the mainland:
- Community organisations were established in major cities
- Cultural events, particularly the annual Coming of the Light celebration (1 July), connected mainland communities to their island heritage
- Family networks maintained connections between mainland and island communities
- Language (Torres Strait Creole and traditional languages) was maintained within community settings
- Traditional dance groups were formed

### The Identity Challenge

The mass migration raised profound questions:
- How to maintain Torres Strait Islander identity when living thousands of kilometres from the homeland
- How to ensure children born on the mainland understood their cultural heritage
- How to sustain island communities when the working-age population was leaving
- How to balance mainland opportunities with cultural obligations in the Torres Strait

### Border No Change Movement

The demographic shift of depopulation in the homelands and migration to the mainland was accompanied by political challenges. When Papua New Guinea achieved independence in 1975, questions arose about the Torres Strait's political future. The **Border No Change movement** unified Islanders across both the strait and the mainland, with Torres Strait Islanders asserting their identity as Australian citizens. This movement demonstrated that even dispersed across the continent, Torres Strait Islanders could mobilise as a unified political community.

The Torres Strait Treaty (signed December 1978, operational February 1985) resolved the border question, establishing a Protected Zone allowing traditional movement across the international boundary.

## Demographic Impact on the Islands

The migration had severe effects on island communities:
- Working-age population was depleted
- Economic activity declined further
- Services became harder to maintain with smaller populations
- Some smaller islands experienced critical population loss
- Between 2016 and 2021, Badu Island's population fell from 813 to 704

However, strong cultural connections, native title recognition, and the Coming of the Light celebrations continue to draw mainland Islanders back to the Torres Strait, maintaining the link between the diaspora and the homelands.
